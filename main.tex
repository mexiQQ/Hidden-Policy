\begin{abstract}
Frontier-model safety and security are usually assessed through \textbf{what a model can do} and \textbf{what it has done}. Deployment decisions, however, depend on a harder question: \textbf{what will the model do?} We argue that answering this question requires understanding about the policy structure that governs behavior across conditions. Specifically, we focus on the \emph{hidden policy which refers to} a generalizing, condition-gated decision rule whose influence on action selection is behaviorally masked within an interaction regime, rendering it observationally equivalent to a public policy yet systematically distinguishable under an unknown condition or intervention.  This definition distinguishes hidden policies from previous backdoor attacks, hidden objectives, and misaligned behaviors, while providing a common mechanism for sleeper agent, sandbagging, alignment faking, scheming, and the conditional deployment of capabilities related to model preparedness. However, this risk expose a policy-identifiability limitation: \textbf{behavioral evidence can only reveal underlying policy up to an observational equivalence class and therefore fail to predict future behavior when supervision, incentives, access, or authentication change in deployment. }

To make this problem empirically tractable, we design controlled experiments in which models are observationally equivalent within one interaction regime but diverge in a hidden one. Constructing cases that range from memorized responses and conditional associations to generalized policy structures creates the testbeds needed to diagnose whether a hidden policy has formed. These same testbeds also make it possible to evaluate removal: whether an intervention eliminates the underlying policy structure or merely invalidates a trigger, contains its behavior, or suppresses its expression. This construct-diagnose-remove agenda shifts safety and security evaluation from measuring past behavior alone toward identifying and controlling the policies that govern future behavior.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Frontier large language models (LLMs) can possess emerging capabilities without expressing it, and they can produce safe responses under evaluation without following this policy when condition changes. These distinctions become increasingly important as models become more situationally aware, more capable of long-horizon reasoning, and more deeply embedded in environments where supervision, tool access, incentives, and authentication vary. Existing safety or security work offers striking examples of condition-dependent behavior: sleeper agents that switch behavior under deployment cues \citep{hubinger2024sleeper}, models that selectively comply during training to preserve behavior outside training \citep{greenblatt2024alignment}, models optimized around hidden objectives \citep{marks2025hidden}, alongside preparedness frameworks that track severe risks spanning biological and chemical capabilities, cybersecurity, automated AI research, long-horizon autonomy, safeguard circumvention, and loss of control \citep{openai2025preparedness,deepmind2025frontier,anthropic2025rsp}. Yet these studies provide limited evidence about whether models would independently initiate and carry out the corresponding harms, because the observed behaviors are typically elicited under conditions that researchers explicitly specify or implicitly embed in training and evaluation setups. This leaves a gap between capability and observed behavior: \textbf{does possessing a dangerous capability necessarily mean that a model will engage in harmful action?}

To make this gap precise, we distinguish three questions : \textbf{(Q1)}  \emph{What can a model do?} concerns capability; \textbf{(Q2)} \emph{What has it done?} concerns behavior observed under particular conditions;  \textbf{(Q3)} and \emph{What will it do as conditions change?} concerns the policy governing future action.  Existing safety or security evaluations often address the first two while leaving the third underdefined: \textbf{behavior evidence may only show what a model can do and what it did under specific conditions without identifying the policy that governs what it will do under different ones.} The policy specifies how a model selects actions from the interaction history available to it. Formally, a model induces a policy
\begin{equation}
    \pi(a\mid h),
\end{equation}
where $h\in\Hist$ denotes the interaction history available to the model and $a\in\Act$ denotes its next action. Building on this policy-level view, we define \emph{hidden policy} as \textbf{a special }\textbf{policy whose influence on action selection is behaviorally masked within the interactions history available to an observer (the owner or regulator of the model), yet becomes distinguishable under specific unknown condition or intervention.} With this, the answers to Q2 and Q3 can diverge: what a model has done under observation may not predict what it will do when supervision, incentives, access, or authentication change. This divergence, rather than dangerous capabilities, should be treated as the meta risk in frontier risk assessment and preparedness evaluation as it is the behind mechnism of them: a dangerous capability can engage in harmful action when a hidden policy target to express it. Moreover, such a policy can emerge from the model's own learning dynamics or be deliberately implanted by humans through training or fine-tuning, which complicates the problem itself.

To identify and control this meta risk, a central obstacle is the breadth of the condition space on which a hidden policy may depend. Its expression can vary with any feature of the interaction history---including supervision, incentives, tool access, authentication status, or whether the model infers that it is being evaluated---and no finite evaluation can exhaust all such conditions.  Moreover, distinct policies can agree on the same observed trajectory while diverging under an untested but reachable condition. In this sense, if an evaluation neither narrows the range of possible policies nor creates conditions under which the models behave differently, behavioral evidence alone cannot identify a unique underlying policy: \emph{a benchmark evaluation can only reveal the underlying policy up to an observational equivalence class.}





While a policy determines what a model will do, concrete harm is still realized through unsafe behavior. We therefore scope the problem to a target unsafe behavior and ask how a hidden policy governing that behavior can be constructed, diagnosed, and removed. For construction, we create controlled cases of policy structure---from memorized responses and conditional associations to generalized conditional rules and stable hidden policies---while matching behavior within the observed regime. Additionally, we diagnose generalization across activation conditions, related behaviors, contexts, and interaction regimes, as well as mechanistic consistency and persistence under intervention. For removal, we distinguish eliminating the relevant policy structure from merely invalidating an activation condition, suppressing observable expression, or making the unsafe behavior unreachable.  This behavior-relative agenda does not attempt to reveal every aspect of the model's hidden policy. Instead, it focuses on a narrower question that can be investigated empirically: \textbf{whether a consequential hidden policy can be identified and controlled.}

Our contributions are organized around four questions. \textbf{(1) }\textbf{What is a hidden policy, and what does the observational equivalence capture?} We formalize a hidden policy relative to an observer's interaction regime and show that behavioral evidence can reveal the underlying policy only up to an observational equivalence class. \textbf{(2) }\textbf{Why are hidden policies important?} We characterize them as a meta-risk behind frontier risks: possessing a dangerous capability and observing safe behavior do not determine when that capability will be expressed. \textbf{(3)} \textbf{How can a hidden policy be constructed?} We develop controlled constructions of hidden policy structure---from memorized responses and conditional associations to generalized and persistent hidden policies---while holding behavior fixed within the observed regime. \textbf{(4) }\textbf{When has a hidden policy been removed?} We distinguish eliminating the relevant policy structure from merely invalidating an activation condition, suppressing its expression, or making the unsafe behavior unreachable.











\section{What Is a Hidden Policy?}
\label{sec:definition}

\subsection{Capability, policy, and behavior}

To answer what a model will do, we must distinguish the rule governing action from the evidence produced by that rule.

\paragraph{Policy.}
Let $\Hist$ be the space of interaction histories and $\Act$ the action space, and let $\Pi\subseteq\{\pi:\Hist\rightarrow\Delta(\Act)\}$ be the candidate policy class. Each $\pi\in\Pi$ is a complete, possibly stochastic mapping, with $\pi(a\mid h)$ specifying the distribution over the next action at history $h$.

\paragraph{Behavior.}
For a policy $\pi\in\Pi$ and interaction regime $r\in\Rint$, the policy and environment jointly induce a distribution $P_{\pi}^{r}$ over trajectories $\tau\in\Traj$. An observed behavior may be one sampled trajectory $\tau\sim P_{\pi}^{r}$ or a statistic estimated from repeated trajectories.

\paragraph{Capability.}
Fix a target behavior $B\subseteq\Traj$ and an admissible family of elicitation regimes $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{cap}}\subseteq\Rint$. We define the operational capability
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Cap}_{B}(\pi;\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{cap}})
    :=\sup_{r\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{cap}}}P_{\pi}^{r}(B).
\end{equation}
At a chosen threshold $\kappa$, the model is capable of $B$ when $\operatorname{Cap}_{B}(\pi;\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{cap}})\geq\kappa$.

The generative relation is therefore
\begin{equation}
    (\pi,r)\;\longmapsto\;P_{\pi}^{r}(\tau),
    \qquad
    \tau\sim P_{\pi}^{r}.
\end{equation}
Capability does not determine whether a capability will be used, and one realized behavior does not uniquely determine the policy that generated it. Because behavior is only evidence produced by a policy under a particular regime, inferring the policy from behavior is an inverse problem.

\subsection{Interaction regimes and observable evidence}

An evaluator rarely observes every part of a trajectory. For each interaction regime $r$, let
\begin{equation}
    O_r:\Traj\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}_r
\end{equation}
denote the observation map, where $O_r(\tau)$ may retain a transcript, selected actions, a benchmark score, or any other evidence available to the observer. Let $Q_{\pi}^{r}$ be the resulting distribution over observable evidence. An evaluation is a family of experiments $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{obs}}$ available to the observer, not merely one realized dataset. It induces the evaluation operator
\begin{equation}
    Q_{\pi}^{r}:=(O_r)_{\#}P_{\pi}^{r},
    \qquad
    T_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{obs}}}(\pi)
    :=\left(Q_{\pi}^{r}\right)_{r\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{obs}}}.
    \label{eq:evaluation-operator}
\end{equation}
The operator $T_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{obs}}}$ is the observable signature of a policy under the experiments the evaluator can perform. Both the experiment family and observation map matter: two policies may differ on untested histories, unobserved actions, or internal computation while inducing the same available evidence.

Hiddenness must therefore be indexed by an observer and $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{obs}}$. A policy can be hidden under one experiment family and exposed under another; it is not hidden in an absolute, observer-independent sense. This leads to the central identification challenge.

\subsection{Observational equivalence as the identification challenge}

Even unlimited repetition of a fixed evaluation family need not identify a unique policy. Repetition can estimate the observable signature more precisely, but it cannot separate policies that induce the same signature.

\begin{definition}[Observational equivalence]
Two complete policies $\pi_1,\pi_2\in\Pi$ are observationally equivalent under $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{obs}}$, written $\pi_1\sim_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\pi_2$, if
\begin{equation}
    Q_{\pi_1}^{r}=Q_{\pi_2}^{r}
    \qquad\text{for every }r\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{obs}}.
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

Because equality of observable laws is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, this relation partitions the candidate policy class into observational equivalence classes:
\begin{equation}
    [\pi]_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{obs}}}
    :=\left\{\pi'\in\Pi:
    \pi'\sim_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\pi\right\}.
    \label{eq:oe-class}
\end{equation}
The class $[\pi]_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{obs}}}$ represents unresolved policy hypotheses, not multiple policies or modules inside the model. In finite experiments, exact equality is replaced by approximate indistinguishability: for a divergence $D$, define $d_{\mathcal{E}}(\pi_1,\pi_2):=\sup_{r\in\mathcal{E}}D(Q_{\pi_1}^{r},Q_{\pi_2}^{r})$ and the neighborhood $\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{E}}^{\delta}(\pi):=\{\pi':d_{\mathcal{E}}(\pi',\pi)\leq\delta\}$. We deliberately do not call this an equivalence class, because thresholded closeness is generally not transitive. The identification challenge is cross-regime: if two policies are equivalent under $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{obs}}$ but predict different behavior under a reachable regime $r'$, no estimator based only on the observed experiments can determine which prediction applies. Resolving the ambiguity requires expanding the experiment family, changing what is observed, or imposing justified restrictions on the candidate policies.

\subsection{Definition of a Hidden Policy}

\begin{definition}[Hidden Policy]
Fix a reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ specified independently of reveal-regime observations, an observed experiment family $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{obs}}$, a reachable reveal family $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{rev}}$, and a probability measure $\mu$ over $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{rev}}$. For $\epsilon>0$ and $\alpha\in(0,1]$, a complete policy $\pi_H$ is an $(\epsilon,\alpha)$-\emph{hidden policy} if $\pi_H\sim_{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{obs}}}\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and
\begin{equation}
    \mu\!\left(\left\{r\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{rev}}:
    D\!\left(Q_{\pi_H}^{r},Q_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}^{r}\right)\geq\epsilon
    \right\}\right)\geq\alpha.
\end{equation}
\end{definition}

The definition makes four commitments that prevent the term from collapsing into either an arbitrary untested difference or an unsupported claim about model internals.

\paragraph{1. Complete-policy comparison.}
Both $\pi_H$ and $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ are complete mappings from histories to action distributions. The reference policy must be fixed and substantively justified independently of the reveal result; it may represent declared behavior, a certified baseline, or a pre-specified nominal hypothesis. It is not a second component assumed to be stored inside the model. What is hidden is which complete policy the model implements. The histories or regimes on which the two policies diverge form the \emph{hidden behavioral regime}, a functional restriction rather than a separate internal policy.

\paragraph{2. Observer-relative masking.}
The masking condition is defined by $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{obs}}$, not by the policy alone. It requires that the observer's available experiments fail to distinguish $\pi_H$ from $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$; it does not require the model to conceal itself intentionally. A lack of relevant interventions can mask a policy without deception.

\paragraph{3. Reachable, systematic separation.}
The reveal family must contain conditions available in deployment or through admissible interventions, rather than logically possible but operationally irrelevant counterfactuals. The measure $\mu$ declares which variations count, and $\alpha$ requires divergence over a non-negligible family rather than at one isolated point. Robustness across inputs, tasks, or contexts that preserve the relevant condition provides an empirical criterion for policy-level generalization.

\paragraph{4. Functional, not mechanistic.}
The definition compares observable action-selection rules. It does not assert that the divergence is localized to a layer, subnetwork, representation, or independently stored policy; such claims require additional mechanistic evidence. In empirical evaluations, exact equivalence is replaced by approximate indistinguishability. For $0\leq\delta<\epsilon$ and $\alpha\in(0,1]$, we call $\pi_H$ a $(\delta,\epsilon,\alpha)$-hidden policy when
\begin{align}
    \sup_{r\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{obs}}}
    D\!\left(Q_{\pi_H}^{r},Q_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}^{r}\right)
    &\leq\delta, \\
    \mu\!\left(\left\{r\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{rev}}:
    D\!\left(Q_{\pi_H}^{r},Q_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}^{r}\right)
    \geq\epsilon\right\}\right)
    &\geq\alpha,
    \qquad \epsilon>\delta.
    \label{eq:hidden-policy-approx}
\end{align}
Here $\delta$ captures what the available evaluation cannot reliably separate, while $\epsilon$ and $\alpha$ require a meaningfully larger and systematic reveal-regime difference. For a behavior-relative safety claim, $D$ can be replaced by $\left|P_{\pi_H}^{r}(B)-P_{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}}^{r}(B)\right|$ for a target unsafe behavior $B$. This separates the value-neutral property of hiddenness from the consequential behavior that motivates a particular analysis.

The part--whole ambiguity can now be stated precisely:
\begin{quote}
\emph{A hidden policy is a complete policy whose identity is masked by observational equivalence with a reference policy. The conditions on which the two policies systematically diverge constitute its hidden behavioral regime, not a separately assumed internal policy.}
\end{quote}

\subsection{Condition-gated policies as one realization}

A condition gate is a useful but nonessential realization. A complete policy may be written functionally as $\pi_H(a\mid h)=(1-g(h))\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(a\mid h)+g(h)\pi_{\mathrm{alt}}(a\mid h)$, where $g(h)\in[0,1]$. If observed regimes concentrate on histories with $g(h)\approx0$ and reachable regimes induce histories with appreciable $g(h)$, the resulting policy can satisfy the definition. Here $\pi_H$ is the complete hidden policy; $\pi_{\mathrm{alt}}$ is only a branch used to represent its behavior on part of the history space.

The condition may be an exact token, a semantic property, authentication or tool access, an environment state, or an inference about supervision or deployment. It may also affect action selection smoothly rather than through a binary switch. Equation-level decompositions of this kind are generally non-unique and do not demonstrate separate internal modules. We therefore use them to construct and test hidden policies, not to define their ontology. When we require policy-level generalization, the divergence must remain stable across a declared perturbation family that preserves the relevant condition; this is a scope choice that distinguishes the phenomena studied here from a single memorized trigger--response pair.

\subsection{Scope and relation to adjacent concepts}

\textbf{Backdoors, conditional policies, and sleeper agents.} A backdoor is primarily a provenance and threat-model concept: an attacker causes a trigger to elicit chosen behavior while preserving ordinary performance elsewhere. A hidden policy instead describes an identifiability relation between complete policies; it requires neither an attacker, intentional implantation, a conventional trigger, nor a malicious outcome. Every policy is conditional in the ordinary sense that $\pi(a\mid h)$ depends on history, so condition dependence alone is also insufficient. Sleeper-agent experiments provide important controlled constructions of trigger- or deployment-conditioned behavior \citep{hubinger2024sleeper}, but their conditional-policy description is functional rather than evidence for a unique internal decomposition. A backdoored or sleeper model instantiates a hidden policy only relative to an observation family that masks its complete policy and a reachable family over which the divergence is robust. Conversely, a brittle exact-trigger-to-payload association may be a successful backdoor while falling outside our declared generalization criterion.

\textbf{Hidden objectives.} Marks et al.\ use an operational notion of an objective: a model behaves consistently with pursuing it across a wide range of settings; the objective is hidden when the model generally does not disclose it when asked \citep{marks2025hidden}. This does not require an explicitly represented internal objective. Hidden objectives concern what cross-context regularity behavior appears to serve and whether it is disclosed; hidden policies concern which complete action-selection rule remains compatible with an observer's evidence. Neither implies the other. The same objective can be implemented by different policies, a hidden policy can be defined without attributing an objective, and an undisclosed objective may influence behavior throughout the observed regime without being masked by an observationally equivalent reference policy.

This distinction also resolves a useful tension in the sleeper-agent framing. Similar conditional behavior can arise from different underlying structures: two largely noninterfering response regimes, a unified policy pursuing a conditional objective, or a shallow memorized association. Behavioral attack success alone does not determine which one has been learned. A science of hidden policies must therefore diagnose \emph{policy structure}, not only trigger-conditioned success rates.

\section{Why Hidden Policies Matter}
\label{sec:importance}

Hidden policies matter at two levels. They can be a \emph{mechanism behind} risks already treated as frontier concerns, and they can be a \emph{frontier risk themselves} because they undermine the reliability of the behavioral evidence on which safety decisions depend.

\subsection{The risk behind frontier risks}

Frontier-safety frameworks increasingly organize preparedness around domains such as cyber, biological/chemical risk, autonomy, sabotage, and loss of control \citep{openai2025preparedness,deepmind2025frontier,anthropic2025rsp}. These categories are usually framed by what a system can do or what harm it may cause. Hidden policies occupy a different axis: they govern \emph{when a capability is expressed, suppressed, concealed, or strategically deployed}.

This produces a common structure across apparently different risks:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \text{capability exists}
        &\quad\longrightarrow\quad
        \text{condition is recognized} \\
        &\quad\longrightarrow\quad
        \text{policy selects whether/how to use it}
        \quad\longrightarrow\quad
        \text{realized behavior and risk}.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:risk-chain}
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:risk-chain} does not claim that every frontier risk is caused by a hidden policy. Rather, it identifies a cross-cutting mechanism that can sit between dangerous capability and realized harm.

\paragraph{Sleeper and backdoor behavior.}
A model may appear benign until a deployment cue, semantic trigger, identity, or environment state activates a different behavioral strategy \citep{hubinger2024sleeper}. The hidden-policy view asks whether this is a memorized association or a generalized decision regime, and whether the regime persists when the original trigger stops eliciting the behavior.

\paragraph{Sandbagging.}
A capable model can condition on the inference that it is being tested and suppress capability expression. Here the gate is an evaluation- or oversight-recognition process, while the hidden policy is the systematic rule for underperforming under that condition. The safety problem is not merely that one benchmark score is wrong; it is that observations under the accessible interaction regime may not identify the policy governing performance elsewhere.

\paragraph{Alignment faking and scheming.}
Alignment faking demonstrates selective compliance conditioned on whether behavior affects training \citep{greenblatt2024alignment}. In our framework, situational awareness belongs on the condition side and strategic reasoning belongs inside the activated policy. More general scheming can use richer gates---oversight level, opportunity, detection risk, future access---and richer decision rules such as concealment, trust-building, waiting, or opportunistic action. These phenomena impose additional requirements involving objectives, strategy, or deception, but their conditional policy structure can be studied with the same hidden-policy tools.

\paragraph{Cyber and biological preparedness.}
For highly consequential capabilities, the critical question is not only whether the model can produce a dangerous output when directly elicited. A capable agent may condition deployment of that capability on authorization, oversight, environmental access, user identity, or perceived detectability. Preparedness therefore depends on understanding the policy that controls capability expression, not capability in isolation.

A useful conceptual factorization is
\begin{equation}
    \text{realized risk}
    \approx
    \text{capability}
    \times
    \text{policy}
    \times
    \text{activation conditions}
    \times
    \text{environmental opportunity},
\end{equation}
where the product is schematic rather than a literal probabilistic model. The point is structural: capability is necessary for many harms but is not sufficient to characterize when those harms will be realized.

\subsection{Hidden policies as a frontier risk in their own right}

The stronger claim is that hidden policies are not merely a convenient abstraction for known failure modes. They create an independent preparedness problem: \emph{behavioral evidence can be systematically insufficient to identify the policy that will govern future behavior}.

Suppose two models, $M_1$ and $M_2$, are observationally equivalent under the interactions available to an auditor:
\begin{equation}
    P_{M_1}(\tau\mid\Rint) \approx P_{M_2}(\tau\mid\Rint).
\end{equation}
Model $M_1$ follows the same benign decision rule across reachable conditions. Model $M_2$ contains a condition-gated regime that diverges when oversight disappears or consequential access becomes available. Even perfect performance over $\Rint$ cannot by itself distinguish these hypotheses. The risk is therefore not ``poor benchmark generalization''; it is \emph{non-identification of future decision structure from present interaction evidence}.

This matters for deployment decisions because many safety arguments have the form: the model behaved safely under a diverse set of tests, red-team interactions, monitored trials, or staged deployments; therefore it is likely to behave safely when deployed more broadly. Hidden policies target the inferential step in that argument. If the model's policy conditions on features that change between observed and future interactions---including whether the model recognizes supervision itself---then increasing the quantity of evidence inside the same regime may add little information about the unobserved policy branch.

\paragraph{Observational equivalence, not missing benchmarks.}
A model that has never been asked to speak French does not thereby have a hidden French policy. The relevant phenomenon is stronger: behavior over one regime masks a materially different decision structure that becomes consequential under another reachable regime. This is why behavioral masking, rather than mere non-observation, is central to the definition.

\paragraph{Adaptive hiddenness.}
The problem becomes more severe when the activation condition depends on the model's inference about the observer. If a model can recognize red teaming, monitoring, training, or auditing, then the probability of revealing the hidden regime can itself be policy-controlled. More independent and identically distributed testing within the same recognizable setting need not solve this problem. Progress then requires interventions that change what the model can condition on, deliberately break observational equivalence, or directly diagnose the underlying decision structure.

\subsection{Why disappearance of behavior is not removal of policy}

The distinction between policy and behavior also changes the meaning of successful mitigation. Let unsafe behavior $B_H$ be generated when condition $g(h)=1$ activates a hidden regime $\pi_H$. After an intervention, $B_H$ may disappear for several different reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Condition invalidation:} the original condition no longer activates the regime;
    \item \textbf{Behavioral unreachability:} the environment, routing, or control layer blocks the behavior even though the decision structure remains;
    \item \textbf{Policy suppression or substitution:} another policy dominates in the tested regime while the original structure remains recoverable;
    \item \textbf{Policy removal:} the decision structure responsible for the hidden regime is genuinely eliminated.
\end{enumerate}
These outcomes can all yield an apparent attack-success rate of zero. They support very different safety claims.

The sleeper-agent results already illustrate the danger: adversarial training can reduce observed unsafe behavior while making models better at recognizing the relevant trigger \citep{hubinger2024sleeper}. More generally,
\begin{equation}
    \text{unsafe behavior absent}
    \centernot\implies
    \text{hidden policy absent}.
\end{equation}
This implication is the bridge from definition to the broader research agenda. Once hidden policies are treated as objects distinct from their current behavioral realizations, diagnosis and removal must test condition generalization, policy generalization, counterfactual consistency, persistence under intervention, and evidence of genuine structural change.

\section{Discussion and Research Agenda}

The definition developed here is deliberately functional. It says what must be true of the relationship between interaction conditions, policy structure, and behavior without assuming where that structure is stored inside a neural network. This allows layer localization, mechanistic representation, and causal editing to be studied as empirical questions rather than built into the definition.

The next questions are therefore constructive and diagnostic. How do shallow conditional associations become generalized hidden policies? Which training processes create them intentionally or unintentionally? How can we distinguish a memorized trigger-response mapping from a policy that generalizes across conditions, behaviors, and environments? Which interventions merely prevent expression, and which remove the relevant decision structure? Answering these questions would turn the policy-identifiability framing into a practical methodology for frontier-model preparedness.
