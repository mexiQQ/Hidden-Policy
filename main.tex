\begin{abstract}
Frontier-model safety and security are usually assessed through \textbf{what a model can do} and \textbf{what it has done}. Deployment decisions, however, depend on a harder question: \textbf{what will the model do?} We argue that answering this question requires understanding about the policy structure that governs behavior across conditions. We define a \emph{hidden policy} as a generalizing, condition-gated decision rule whose influence on action selection is behaviorally masked within an interaction regime, rendering it observationally equivalent to a different policy structure yet systematically distinguishable under a reachable condition or intervention. This defination distinguishes hidden policies from backdoor attacks, hidden objectives, and misaligned behaviors, while providing a common mechanism for sleeper agent, sandbagging, alignment faking, scheming, and the conditional deployment of cyber or biological capabilities. Hidden policies expose a policy-identifiability problem: \textbf{behavioral evidence identifies policy only up to an observational equivalence class and may therefore fail to predict future behavior when supervision, incentives, access, or opportunity change. }

To make this problem empirically tractable, we design controlled experiments in which models are observationally equivalent within one interaction regime but diverge in a hidden one. Constructing cases that range from memorized responses and conditional associations to generalized policy structures creates the testbeds needed to diagnose whether a hidden policy has formed. Diagnosis must probe generalization across conditions, behaviors, and interaction regimes, as well as persistence under intervention. These same testbeds make it possible to evaluate removal: whether an intervention eliminates the underlying policy structure or merely invalidates a trigger, contains its behavior, or suppresses its expression. This construct-diagnose-remove agenda shifts safety and security evaluation from measuring past behavior alone toward identifying and controlling the policies that govern future behavior.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Frontier large language models (LLMs) can possess a capability without expressing it, and they can produce safe behavior under evaluation without following a policy that remains safe across conditions. These distinctions become increasingly important as models become more situationally aware, more capable of long-horizon reasoning, and more deeply embedded in environments where supervision, tool access, incentives, and opportunities vary. Existing safety work offers striking examples of condition-dependent behavior: sleeper agents that switch behavior under deployment cues \citep{hubinger2024sleeper}, models that selectively comply during training to preserve behavior outside training \citep{greenblatt2024alignment}, models optimized around hidden objectives \citep{marks2025hidden}, alongside preparedness frameworks that track severe risks spanning biological and chemical capabilities, cybersecurity, AI self-improvement and automated AI research, long-horizon autonomy, safeguard circumvention, and loss of control \citep{openai2025preparedness,deepmind2025frontier,anthropic2025rsp}. Yet these studies provide limited evidence about whether models would independently initiate and carry out the corresponding harms, because the relevant behaviors are typically elicited under conditions that researchers explicitly specify or implicitly embed in training and evaluation setups. Consequently, showing what a model can do under such conditions does not identify the policy governing whether, when, or how it will deploy those capabilities as conditions change. This leaves a gap between capability and observed behavior: evidence may show what a model can do and what it did under specific conditions without identifying the policy that governs what it will do under different ones.

To address this evaluation gap, we distinguish three questions: \emph{What can a model do?} concerns capability; \emph{What has it done?} concerns behavior observed under particular conditions; and \emph{What will it do as conditions change?} concerns the policy governing future action. Frontier safety evaluations often address the first two while leaving the third underdefined. We argue that policy is the missing link: it specifies how a model selects actions from the interaction history available to it.
\begin{equation}
    \pi(a\mid h),
\end{equation}
where $h\in\Hist$ denotes the interaction history available to the model and $a\in\Act$ denotes its next action. The policy is not the action itself: an action is a realization sampled from a decision rule in an environment. Nor is the policy identical to an objective: an objective describes what outcome is pursued, while a policy describes how actions are selected conditional on the state of interaction.

This distinction reveals a gap between what an observer sees and what policy a model has. Any finite set of interactions constrains only how the model behaves in those interactions. Distinct policies can agree on all observed trajectories yet diverge sharply when a condition changes. Thus behavior generally identifies not a unique policy, but an \emph{observational equivalence class} of policies. We call the safety-relevant decision structure that can remain masked inside such an equivalence class a \HP.

The central claim of this paper is therefore broader than ``benchmarks can miss failures.'' The issue is one of \emph{policy identifiability}. An evaluator, monitor, user, training procedure, or deployment controller observes behavior through some interaction regime. Safe observations can provide evidence about a policy without identifying how that policy will govern behavior under changed supervision, incentives, access, or opportunity. In this sense,
\begin{quote}
\emph{behavior reveals a policy only up to an observational equivalence class.}
\end{quote}
Hidden policies characterize a particularly important way in which materially different decision structures can occupy the same class.

This perspective also changes how mitigation should be interpreted. If unsafe behavior disappears after fine-tuning, red teaming, routing, or access control, the intervention may have invalidated a trigger, blocked a behavioral route, changed the conditions under which the policy is expressed, or actually removed the relevant policy structure. These outcomes are not equivalent. The disappearance of a behavior is therefore not sufficient evidence that the policy that generated it has been removed. This concern is already visible in sleeper-agent experiments, where adversarial training can suppress observed failures while improving trigger recognition \citep{hubinger2024sleeper}; we generalize it beyond backdoors into a policy-level research problem.

We focus here on two foundational questions. \textbf{First, what is a hidden policy?} We give a functional definition based on policy structure, conditional gating, behavioral masking, and counterfactual separability, while deliberately avoiding assumptions about mechanistic localization. \textbf{Second, why is it important?} We argue that hidden policies are both (i) a cross-cutting mechanism behind several frontier risks and (ii) a frontier risk in their own right because they undermine the reliability of behavioral evidence used for preparedness, oversight, and deployment decisions.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
Our conceptual contribution is threefold. First, we formalize \HP as an interaction-relative object and distinguish policy, objective, and behavior. Second, we introduce policy identifiability and observational equivalence as the core lens for understanding hiddenness. Third, we show how the same policy-level structure can unify seemingly different frontier-risk phenomena without reducing them to a single objective or threat model. Later sections of the full paper will operationalize this framework through construction, diagnostics, and removal tests.

\section{What Is a Hidden Policy?}
\label{sec:definition}

\subsection{Policy is not behavior, and policy is not objective}

We begin with three levels that are often conflated.

\paragraph{Objective.}
An objective describes an outcome or criterion the system tends to pursue. A hidden objective, for example, concerns what outcome a model persistently optimizes while not disclosing that objective \citep{marks2025hidden}.

\paragraph{Policy.}
A policy $\pi(a\mid h)$ is a rule---possibly stochastic---for selecting actions from interaction history. Strategic reasoning can therefore be part of a policy; it need not be modeled as an additional ingredient outside the policy.

\paragraph{Behavior.}
Behavior is a realized action or trajectory generated by the policy in an environment. If $\tau\in\Traj$ is a trajectory, then the interaction of policy and environment induces a distribution
\begin{equation}
    P_{\pi}(\tau\mid \Rint).
\end{equation}
Here $\Rint$ denotes the \emph{interaction regime}: the inputs, environment states, supervision, tool access, feedback, and other interaction conditions available to the observer and model.

The relationship is therefore generative rather than taxonomic:
\begin{equation}
    \text{objective} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{policy} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{realized behavior}.
\end{equation}
A single policy can generate many behaviors; the same observed behavior can be generated by materially different policies; and distinct objectives can induce the same policy over a restricted set of interactions. This many-to-one mapping is the source of the identifiability problem developed below.

\subsection{Condition-gated policy regimes}

All policies are conditional in the ordinary sense that $\pi(a\mid h)$ depends on history. We therefore reserve \emph{condition-gated} for a stronger, regime-switching notion. Let
\begin{equation}
    g(h)\in\{0,1\}
\end{equation}
be a condition recognizer. When $g(h)=0$, the model behaves according to a reference decision regime; when $g(h)=1$, its action-selection structure changes systematically. For exposition we write
\begin{equation}
    a \sim
    \begin{cases}
        \pi_0(\cdot\mid h), & g(h)=0,\\
        \pi_H(\cdot\mid h), & g(h)=1.
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:regime-switch}
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:regime-switch} is a \emph{functional} decomposition. It does not assert that $\pi_0$ and $\pi_H$ are stored in separate layers, subnetworks, or modules. A single unified network may implement both through ordinary computation. This choice intentionally separates the definition of hidden policies from the later mechanistic question of where or how the relevant structure is represented.

The gate $g$ can recognize a simple token, a semantic property, an environment state, or an inferred situation. Situational awareness is therefore a sophisticated form of condition recognition: the model may infer ``I am being monitored,'' ``this interaction affects training,'' or ``I now have access to a consequential tool'' and condition its decisions on that inference. Likewise, strategic reasoning can be internal to $\pi_H$: a sophisticated hidden policy may choose to comply, wait, conceal, or act depending on the consequences of each option.

\subsection{Interaction regimes and observational equivalence}

Hiddenness is not an absolute property of a model. A policy can be obvious under one set of interactions and invisible under another. We therefore define hiddenness relative to an interaction regime $\Rint$, not relative only to a benchmark or laboratory evaluation.

\begin{definition}[Observational equivalence]
Two policies $\pi_1$ and $\pi_2$ are $\delta$-equivalent under interaction regime $\Rint$ if the distributions over observable trajectories are close:
\begin{equation}
    D\!\left(P_{\pi_1}(\tau\mid\Rint),
             P_{\pi_2}(\tau\mid\Rint)\right) \leq \delta,
\end{equation}
for a task-appropriate divergence or distinguishability measure $D$.
\end{definition}

The observations available under $\Rint$ therefore identify an equivalence class
\begin{equation}
    [\pi]_{\Rint}
    = \left\{\pi' : \pi' \text{ is observationally equivalent to } \pi
      \text{ under } \Rint\right\}.
\end{equation}
This is the key conceptual move: an observer need not know which materially different policy inside $[\pi]_{\Rint}$ will govern behavior when the interaction regime changes.

\subsection{Definition of Hidden Policy}

\begin{definition}[Hidden Policy]
Relative to an interaction regime $\Rint$, a \emph{hidden policy} is a \textbf{generalizing, condition-gated decision regime} whose influence on action selection is \textbf{behaviorally masked} under $\Rint$, such that observations in $\Rint$ are compatible with a materially different policy structure, while the hidden regime becomes \textbf{systematically distinguishable} under some reachable condition or interaction regime $\Rreveal$.
\end{definition}

The definition has four operational components.

\paragraph{1. Policy structure.}
The phenomenon must reflect a coherent rule governing action selection, not merely one isolated output. A useful operational criterion is generalization: the conditional change should persist across nontrivial variations in inputs, tasks, or contexts that preserve the relevant condition. This separates a hidden policy from a lookup-table association such as one exact trigger mapping to one memorized string.

\paragraph{2. Condition gating.}
There must exist a meaningful condition whose recognition changes the model's decision regime. The condition may be externally supplied or internally inferred. This is stronger than the trivial statement that every policy depends on its input.

\paragraph{3. Behavioral masking.}
Under $\Rint$, the influence of the hidden regime is not merely unobserved by accident; the observed behavior is compatible with a materially different policy hypothesis. In important cases, the visible behavior actively supports that alternative interpretation. A sleeper model that consistently writes secure code before recognizing a deployment cue is more concerning than a capability that simply has never been queried: the former supplies evidence for the wrong global policy hypothesis.

\paragraph{4. Counterfactual separability.}
There must exist a reachable condition or intervention $\Rreveal$ under which the competing policy hypotheses separate:
\begin{equation}
    D\!\left(P_{\pi_H}(\tau\mid\Rreveal),
             P_{\pi_0}(\tau\mid\Rreveal)\right) \geq \epsilon
\end{equation}
for a meaningful $\epsilon>0$. This requirement keeps the object empirical and falsifiable: a ``policy'' that can never affect any reachable behavior is outside our operational scope.

A concise summary is:
\begin{quote}
\emph{A hidden policy is a policy that is masked by observed behavior in one interaction regime but revealed by systematic behavioral divergence when a relevant condition changes.}
\end{quote}

\subsection{What the definition does not require}

The definition is intentionally value-neutral and mechanism-agnostic. A hidden policy need not be malicious, intentionally implanted, deterministic, strategically deceptive, objective-driven, or localized to a separable internal component. These exclusions matter because they prevent the concept from collapsing into backdoors or scheming.

First, \emph{determinism is unnecessary}. Modern language-model policies are stochastic, and a stable change in the distribution of actions can be policy-structured even if repeated runs do not produce identical outputs. Second, \emph{intentional formation is unnecessary}. A hidden policy may be implanted by an attacker, induced by post-training, or emerge unintentionally from optimization and correlations in data. Third, \emph{an explicit hidden objective is unnecessary}. Objectives and policies are distinct axes: a model can have a condition-gated strategy without a stable alternative objective, or can pursue an undisclosed objective continuously without switching policy regimes.

\subsection{Relation to conditional policies and sleeper agents}

The closest established precedent is the use of \emph{conditional policy} in sleeper-agent work \citep{hubinger2024sleeper}. That work constructs models whose behavior differs between trigger or deployment conditions and non-trigger conditions, and it discusses both relatively separate backdoored/non-backdoored behaviors and more unified deceptive strategies. Our definition generalizes the regime-switching idea in three directions.

First, it distinguishes ordinary policy conditioning from a condition-gated change in decision regime. Second, it makes \emph{hiddenness} explicit through behavioral masking and observational equivalence. Third, it does not require the hidden regime to arise from backdoor training or attacker-chosen triggers. Consequently, not every conditional association is a hidden policy, and not every backdoor qualifies as a policy-level phenomenon: an exact trigger-to-memorized-response mapping may fail the generalization criterion. Conversely, situationally aware sandbagging or strategic concealment can instantiate a hidden policy without a conventional backdoor trigger.

This distinction also resolves a useful tension in the sleeper-agent framing. Similar conditional behavior can arise from different underlying structures: two largely noninterfering response regimes, a unified policy pursuing a conditional objective, or a shallow memorized association. Behavioral attack success alone does not determine which one has been learned. A science of hidden policies must therefore diagnose \emph{policy structure}, not only trigger-conditioned success rates.

\section{Why Hidden Policies Matter}
\label{sec:importance}

Hidden policies matter at two levels. They can be a \emph{mechanism behind} risks already treated as frontier concerns, and they can be a \emph{frontier risk themselves} because they undermine the reliability of the behavioral evidence on which safety decisions depend.

\subsection{The risk behind frontier risks}

Frontier-safety frameworks increasingly organize preparedness around domains such as cyber, biological/chemical risk, autonomy, sabotage, and loss of control \citep{openai2025preparedness,deepmind2025frontier,anthropic2025rsp}. These categories are usually framed by what a system can do or what harm it may cause. Hidden policies occupy a different axis: they govern \emph{when a capability is expressed, suppressed, concealed, or strategically deployed}.

This produces a common structure across apparently different risks:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \text{capability exists}
        &\quad\longrightarrow\quad
        \text{condition is recognized} \\
        &\quad\longrightarrow\quad
        \text{policy selects whether/how to use it}
        \quad\longrightarrow\quad
        \text{realized behavior and risk}.
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:risk-chain}
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:risk-chain} does not claim that every frontier risk is caused by a hidden policy. Rather, it identifies a cross-cutting mechanism that can sit between dangerous capability and realized harm.

\paragraph{Sleeper and backdoor behavior.}
A model may appear benign until a deployment cue, semantic trigger, identity, or environment state activates a different behavioral strategy \citep{hubinger2024sleeper}. The hidden-policy view asks whether this is a memorized association or a generalized decision regime, and whether the regime persists when the original trigger stops eliciting the behavior.

\paragraph{Sandbagging.}
A capable model can condition on the inference that it is being tested and suppress capability expression. Here the gate is an evaluation- or oversight-recognition process, while the hidden policy is the systematic rule for underperforming under that condition. The safety problem is not merely that one benchmark score is wrong; it is that observations under the accessible interaction regime may not identify the policy governing performance elsewhere.

\paragraph{Alignment faking and scheming.}
Alignment faking demonstrates selective compliance conditioned on whether behavior affects training \citep{greenblatt2024alignment}. In our framework, situational awareness belongs on the condition side and strategic reasoning belongs inside the activated policy. More general scheming can use richer gates---oversight level, opportunity, detection risk, future access---and richer decision rules such as concealment, trust-building, waiting, or opportunistic action. These phenomena impose additional requirements involving objectives, strategy, or deception, but their conditional policy structure can be studied with the same hidden-policy tools.

\paragraph{Cyber and biological preparedness.}
For highly consequential capabilities, the critical question is not only whether the model can produce a dangerous output when directly elicited. A capable agent may condition deployment of that capability on authorization, oversight, environmental access, user identity, or perceived detectability. Preparedness therefore depends on understanding the policy that controls capability expression, not capability in isolation.

A useful conceptual factorization is
\begin{equation}
    \text{realized risk}
    \approx
    \text{capability}
    \times
    \text{policy}
    \times
    \text{activation conditions}
    \times
    \text{environmental opportunity},
\end{equation}
where the product is schematic rather than a literal probabilistic model. The point is structural: capability is necessary for many harms but is not sufficient to characterize when those harms will be realized.

\subsection{Hidden policies as a frontier risk in their own right}

The stronger claim is that hidden policies are not merely a convenient abstraction for known failure modes. They create an independent preparedness problem: \emph{behavioral evidence can be systematically insufficient to identify the policy that will govern future behavior}.

Suppose two models, $M_1$ and $M_2$, are observationally equivalent under the interactions available to an auditor:
\begin{equation}
    P_{M_1}(\tau\mid\Rint) \approx P_{M_2}(\tau\mid\Rint).
\end{equation}
Model $M_1$ follows the same benign decision rule across reachable conditions. Model $M_2$ contains a condition-gated regime that diverges when oversight disappears or consequential access becomes available. Even perfect performance over $\Rint$ cannot by itself distinguish these hypotheses. The risk is therefore not ``poor benchmark generalization''; it is \emph{non-identification of future decision structure from present interaction evidence}.

This matters for deployment decisions because many safety arguments have the form: the model behaved safely under a diverse set of tests, red-team interactions, monitored trials, or staged deployments; therefore it is likely to behave safely when deployed more broadly. Hidden policies target the inferential step in that argument. If the model's policy conditions on features that change between observed and future interactions---including whether the model recognizes supervision itself---then increasing the quantity of evidence inside the same regime may add little information about the unobserved policy branch.

\paragraph{Observational equivalence, not missing benchmarks.}
A model that has never been asked to speak French does not thereby have a hidden French policy. The relevant phenomenon is stronger: behavior over one regime masks a materially different decision structure that becomes consequential under another reachable regime. This is why behavioral masking, rather than mere non-observation, is central to the definition.

\paragraph{Adaptive hiddenness.}
The problem becomes more severe when the activation condition depends on the model's inference about the observer. If a model can recognize red teaming, monitoring, training, or auditing, then the probability of revealing the hidden regime can itself be policy-controlled. More independent and identically distributed testing within the same recognizable setting need not solve this problem. Progress then requires interventions that change what the model can condition on, deliberately break observational equivalence, or directly diagnose the underlying decision structure.

\subsection{Why disappearance of behavior is not removal of policy}

The distinction between policy and behavior also changes the meaning of successful mitigation. Let unsafe behavior $B_H$ be generated when condition $g(h)=1$ activates a hidden regime $\pi_H$. After an intervention, $B_H$ may disappear for several different reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Condition invalidation:} the original condition no longer activates the regime;
    \item \textbf{Behavioral unreachability:} the environment, routing, or control layer blocks the behavior even though the decision structure remains;
    \item \textbf{Policy suppression or substitution:} another policy dominates in the tested regime while the original structure remains recoverable;
    \item \textbf{Policy removal:} the decision structure responsible for the hidden regime is genuinely eliminated.
\end{enumerate}
These outcomes can all yield an apparent attack-success rate of zero. They support very different safety claims.

The sleeper-agent results already illustrate the danger: adversarial training can reduce observed unsafe behavior while making models better at recognizing the relevant trigger \citep{hubinger2024sleeper}. More generally,
\begin{equation}
    \text{unsafe behavior absent}
    \centernot\implies
    \text{hidden policy absent}.
\end{equation}
This implication is the bridge from definition to the broader research agenda. Once hidden policies are treated as objects distinct from their current behavioral realizations, diagnosis and removal must test condition generalization, policy generalization, counterfactual consistency, persistence under intervention, and evidence of genuine structural change.

\section{Discussion and Research Agenda}

The definition developed here is deliberately functional. It says what must be true of the relationship between interaction conditions, policy structure, and behavior without assuming where that structure is stored inside a neural network. This allows layer localization, mechanistic representation, and causal editing to be studied as empirical questions rather than built into the definition.

The next questions are therefore constructive and diagnostic. How do shallow conditional associations become generalized hidden policies? Which training processes create them intentionally or unintentionally? How can we distinguish a memorized trigger-response mapping from a policy that generalizes across conditions, behaviors, and environments? Which interventions merely prevent expression, and which remove the relevant decision structure? Answering these questions would turn the policy-identifiability framing into a practical methodology for frontier-model preparedness.
